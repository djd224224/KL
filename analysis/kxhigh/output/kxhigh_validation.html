1 · Forecast accuracy

What this section measuresHow close our weather forecasts came to the actual high temperature in each city. We aggregate forecasts from the National Weather Service (NWS) and Weather Underground (AccuWeather is dead — API key expired). MAE (mean absolute error) = typical miss in °F. Bias = systematic over/under forecast (near zero is ideal). Cities where our forecast is consistently off are cities where betting is riskier.
Takeaways
  • Overall forecast MAE is 1.68°F — typical for next-day max forecasts. The ensemble runs cold (under-predicts) (bias -1.00°F).
  • GFS MAE 1.20°F vs ECMWF MAE 2.71°F — GFS edges out by 1.51°F. When they disagree, that spread is usable signal.
  • Hardest to forecast (largest MAE): AUS (2.5°F), TBOS (2.5°F). Easiest: TLV (0.8°F), TOKC (1.1°F).
  • Cities with material systematic bias: TBOS (-2.5°F, forecasts too cold), MIA (-2.0°F, forecasts too cold). Worth a regime-specific correction.

Forecast source: 82 from bot snapshots, 304 from Open-Meteo historical backfill (GFS + ECMWF ensemble).

2 · Prediction calibration

What this section measuresWhen the model says "70% chance YES," does YES actually happen 70% of the time? Brier score is the report card (lower = better; 0.25 = coin flip; 0.0 = perfect). The reliability diagram plots predicted probability vs. actual outcome rate — points on the dashed diagonal mean the model is well-calibrated. Below the line = overconfident; above = underconfident. We also compare our model’s Brier to what the market itself was implying, to see if we have genuine edge or we’re just agreeing with the crowd.
Takeaways
  • Model Brier score: 0.244 (poor). Baseline is 0.25 (coin flip); perfect is 0.000. Log loss: 1.516. Sample size: n=90 — reasonable.
  • ⚠ Market beats the model: 0.244 vs 0.217 (-0.027). The bot's probability estimates are less accurate than what the market already prices in — edge is unclear.

4 · Edge capture (per fill)

What this section measuresFor every single trade, "edge" is the gap between what the model thought was fair and what we actually paid. A +10% edge means the model thought we were getting a 10 percentage point discount relative to fair odds. The question: do higher-edge trades actually pay better? If the scatter trends up-right and the quintile table shows higher P&L in high-edge buckets, the model’s edge signal is real. If not, the edge is mostly noise.
Takeaways
  • Mean P&L per fill: lowest-edge quintile $-0.66, highest-edge $-2.87. ⚠ Edge signal is inverted — fills the model thought were best edge actually lose more. Suggests the model's fair-price calculation is biased in the direction it thinks is profitable.
  • Best edge bin: (0.08, 0.218] → $+2.47/fill avg P&L, 58% win rate on 93 fills.
  • ⚠ Only 467 fills have snapshot context (the rest fall in the Mar 11 → Apr 18 logging gap). This analysis is preliminary until snapshot data accumulates.

5 · Execution quality

What this section measuresEven a great model can be ruined by bad execution. This section grades how the bot places and fills orders, not its forecasting. The bot posts limit orders and waits (never pays the spread), so fill rate is low by design (~3%) — the tradeoff is better prices. Key checks: Are we getting filled disproportionately on bad prices? Are fees eating returns? Are maker fills (we posted) more profitable than taker fills (we chased)?
Takeaways
  • Fill rate: 3.5% of orders and 1.9% of contracts across 36,421 placed orders. Low is expected for a post-only bot.
  • Fees: $1.49 (0.4% of gross) — negligible. Net P&L $416.48 vs gross $417.97.
  • All fills are maker (post-only). No takers — as designed.
  • Highest fill rate cities: LAX (3.7%), MIA (3.0%). Lowest: SATX (0.5%), SFO (0.5%). Low fill rate ≠ bad — often means our limit prices are conservative.

Takeaways

7 · Filter sensitivity (distance + source agreement)

What this section measuresTwo filter ideas tested as counterfactuals: would P&L improve if we skipped certain markets at the time of trade? Distance filter targets bucket centers very close to the forecast μ — markets where the model is confident, fair NO is low, and the bot tends to overpay relative to fair (the 4/23 loss pattern). Source agreement filter targets markets where NWS and WU agree tightly — same signal from a different angle. For each threshold, we compute the P&L of the markets that survive the filter (= markets we would have traded). Higher than control = filter helps; lower = filter cuts profitable markets too.
Takeaways
  • Distance filter best: threshold = 1.0°F (keeps 33 of 80 markets, Δ = $+325.07 vs control $501.30).
  • Agreement filter best: threshold = ≤5.0°F (keeps 80 of 80 markets, Δ = $+0.00 vs subset control $501.30).
  • Per-city pattern: distance filter helps the loss-prone cities (Miami, Phoenix, Houston, NOLA, Philadelphia) and hurts the win-prone ones (Austin, LA, Seattle). Same structural trade-off as the A/B treatment cap.
  • Caution: these are in-sample optima on a still-small dataset. Bootstrap CIs (computed in analysis/kxhigh/python/backtest_filters.py) are wide enough that none of these filter levels reach statistical significance yet. Re-evaluate after 4-6 weeks of accumulated fills.
